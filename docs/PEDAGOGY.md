# Pedagogy principles

## Teach through a spiral

Do not organise the course as one long block per topic or as a rigid month-by-month calendar. Introduce a manageable part of a concept, practise it in useful subjects, revisit it later, combine it with other skills, and eventually invite independent application. Each lesson's fundamental records use the stages `introduced`, `practised`, `revisited`, `combined`, and `independent` (British spelling is intentional).

Sequence prerequisites explicitly, but do not make every later subject wait for complete mastery of one isolated skill. Repetition should change context, complexity, material, or learner choice so it extends learning rather than repeating a prompt.

## Make drawing the main activity

A normal lesson should take about 30–60 minutes. Its 5–10 minute warm-up should prepare the same observation or motor skill used in the main drawing. Explain only enough theory to make the exercise possible. Give the learner time to make a recognisable drawing, then use a few targeted questions to notice evidence and choose what to practise next. Optional extensions may deepen or vary the exercise.

Support artist studies, master studies, experiments, review/checkpoints, longer projects, and a final project in addition to normal lessons. The schema distinguishes lesson kinds without requiring every type to use the same exact exercise shape.

## Vary subjects and increase independence

Use objects and scenes from life (for example, mugs, fruit, books, shoes, furniture, plants, flowers, hands, rooms, windows, and outdoor subjects), supplied references (including portrait, figure, animal, landscape, lighting, and perspective references), genuine artworks for master studies, and gradually increasing learner choice. Record subject type, source, and scaffolding so future audits can check whether the learner is relying on one kind of reference or instruction.

Stylised drawing, including Pokémon, can make construction, proportion, gesture, design, lighting, and environment personally engaging. It may occasionally reinforce fundamentals, but especially early on it must not replace observational and structural drawing practice.

## Materials, art history, and personal style

Begin mainly with graphite. Introduce charcoal later when broad value, light and shadow, edges, gesture, portraiture, atmosphere, or expressive marks benefit from it. Colour painting is outside this course.

Integrate art history with drawing ideas such as line, value, composition, atmosphere, form, portraiture, simplification, and expressive drawing. Use genuine works for master studies and preserve artwork provenance. Do not prescribe a personal style; create opportunities for the learner to develop preferences through artists, study, experimentation, media, composition, mark-making, interpretation, and independence.

## Review for learning

Use self-review to direct attention, not to grade the learner's worth or enforce a single correct style. Ask concrete questions about what was observed, what changed, what reads clearly, and what to try next. Local progress history can record completion, revisits, concept practice, and settings; later practice-mode choices should reflect concepts already encountered without overbuilding that feature in Phase 1.
